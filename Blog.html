19 March 2026 Theory Derivation

Precession Frequency — Ω = τ/L

Defined precession formally: the motion of the axis of a spinning body tracing a cone, caused by the action of an external torque. The key term to introduce here is the precession frequency Ω.

Ω = τ / L

where τ = applied torque, L = angular momentum

The ratio of torque to angular momentum. Higher spin → higher L → for the same torque, Ω is smaller. The precession is less significant. The rocket stays on trajectory.

Conversely, a slow-spinning rocket has less L. The same aerodynamic torque produces a much larger Ω — significant precession — which can lead to unstable tumbling.

Key insight

The Thanjavur Bommai analogy: push it sideways and it doesn't fall — it wobbles and returns. The spin is the base. Angular momentum resists the fall. The faster the spin, the tighter the wobble.

Also noted: Is and IR are not defined here again — carried forward from JEE knowledge. Stability requires Is << IR. For long thin rockets this holds naturally. Short thin rockets have comparable Is and IR — and are unstable.

24 February 2026 Theory

Direction, not magnitude.

The question posed: if a torque τ acts perpendicular to a rotating body's angular momentum L, what changes — magnitude, direction, or both?

The answer is direction only. When τ ⊥ L, the vectors are perpendicular. The torque adds a small piece to L at right angles — so L rotates its direction without changing length. This is precession.

τ = dL/dt

if τ ⊥ L → |L| constant, direction changes

The intuition: think of L as an arrow. The torque is another arrow attached perpendicular to it. Moving the torque just rotates the direction of L — it traces a cone. This is non-intuitive but geometrically exact.

23 February 2026 Errors Theory

Errors in my assumptions.

First wrong assumption documented: I thought 'I' (moment of inertia) alone was the main factor for stability. My reasoning was that low I → more ω for the same L → faster spin → more stable. This came from S.K. Das.

Fallacy

I forgot angular momentum isn't conserved when external torques act.

I also ignored that there are actually two moments of inertia — Is (along spin axis) and IR (along rotation axis). Reducing Is alone is not the only factor.

The corrected picture: for long, thin rockets, Is is small and IR is large — this is the stable configuration. For short thin rockets, Is and IR are comparable — unstable.

Stability condition: Is << IR

Long thin rocket → Is small, IR large → stable ✓

Short thin rocket → Is ≈ IR → unstable ✗

Reducing Is is necessary but not sufficient. The ratio Is/IR is what governs stability — which points toward precession as the next concept to understand.
